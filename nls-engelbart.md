# Engelbart and the oN-Line System
### An introduction
The oN-Line System was developed in the 1960s by Douglas Engelbart and his Augmentation Research Center, hosted by Stanford Research Institute. Its admittedly trippy special session, running for an hour and 40 minutes roughly, changed the landscape of computing forever, and had since become known as the Mother of All Demos. It showed prototypical versions of many things that we do today: hyperlinking, file searching, gain control over text, cooperative control of systems and data, the works! It even came with the first mouse in practical use!

Since then, under his purview, Augment was developed, a mainframe application used in the DEC line with the same exact purpose in mind, with numerous GUI improvements as text-mode environments started to become more complex. It later received an IBM-PC DOS emulation version, which Engelbart used to continue his work until the development of Hyperscope, a web-based version of the same principles.

He had died a few years ago at the time of writing, rest his soul. Everyone who is still here, whether from his old ARC team or the Hyperscope team, are pressing on however, knowing that in the end, the augmentation, not replacement, of human intellect is the way to go. As a Christian, it alarms me to see AI take over the way they have, and it warms my heart to see humanity still fight to better itself in the face of unwanted wars, dark forces and psychopaths.
### The controls of the NLS
Until the release of Hyperscope, which uses shortcuts, the NLS and Augment program used a 5-button (or piano key if you prefer) input device which can, through chords, input all lower and upper-case Latin characters used in the English alphabet. This is used to both input text on the fly and issue commands to the terminal which you are logged on to.

Also, it's the first timesharing system specifically using a graphics user interface, or GUI, compared to most timesharing systems like the DEC or IBM operating systems before it. On the top, you see the status of the system, the inputs it can accept at that time for the file, whether you've shared a "blackboard" with another user and so on. Just below it is the line, column and ID of the statement you have clicked or jumped to.

When processing text, you can access all texts and programs at once, or just the open document, when searching or jumping, whether to an ID that you are certain exists or to a word that you know is in the document in question. You can also do limited graphics, which can be programmed to make all sorts of things, like low-poly lineart and multiple types of graphs. This was powerful in the hands of people who wanted to boost the efficiency of their brains.

When collaborating with others, you can access the built-in telecoms net and request permission to access the other user's terminal. Once you enter the terminal, both mouse cursors will show up, but the owner of the terminal has a little more administrative control, which can help if their guest turns unruly. Once you're done, you can disconnect and show your terminal's contents again.

This presumably required an insane level of trust, though ARC did host open house sessons for people to learn and tinker with the systems in question, maybe with a different network entirely.
