(I'm going without my Nostromo for this one. Phone needs charging. Much slower to spin up.)

Many decentralization efforts have happened over the past 25-30 years, all of them good for one reason or another, and all of them are good because it splits up the command structure all the more. I'm going to talk about some of the ones I've used, and then the ones I've learned about. ## Services I use and used
### Matrix
Matrix is an encrypted room messaging service with federation, with most clients supporting direct messages with both participants as admins of the room. I used to use Matrix's official instance, but after realizing that it's unironically centralized, I went to self-hosted services like Calitabby. While I did have a single run-in with the cat himself over human biology, the server is largely chill, and may have been my best choice of server so far.

Because it's encrypted, the issue becomes not losing your encryption keys. Those are needed to decrypt messages in rooms and DMs that are encrypted, and you'll lose all chat history (or parts of the chat history if your keys end up convoluted). If you need to keep prying eyes off you, this is better than most.

*Be warned,* if you're an administrator, moderating is somewhat harder to do here.

### Tox
Tox is, simply put, a forgetful anonymous instant messenger with its own encryption. This is useful for people who NEED to speak out but are in danger of getting decimated if they do, such as *real* journalists, whistleblowers, and anyone whose information could cause trouble for criminals and tyrants. If you just want to talk to people, you CAN do that, it's just less useful for that unlike IRC.

### IRC
Speaking of IRC, I used it before when I was wanting the skinny on the Avara PC port project. It's simple, stupidly simple, and to this day, no IRC client has ever phoned home save for one or two which got quickly ousted from the community. You have instant messaging and channels, fit for everyday use, and if you're an admin, you have a lot of moderation tools at your disposal.

### Nostr
Nostr is an alternative federated social media infrastructure that uses blockchains to prevent easy censorship of information that is posted there, except when the user wants it removed. I used it a bit on my iPhone, each instance has their own rules, but I just keep a general set in mind aligning with my moral code. It's a bit like Twitter or Facebook in that it's infinitely scrolling, and you can either like or dislike a post, as well as comment on it.

Where it shines is how easily you can filter content to your preferences, especially when politics distress you and you just want to focus on family, for example.

### Mastodon
I have never signed up to a Mastodon instance, but I HAVE been to a few instances to read about some things that caught my attention. It's much more like Twitter in structure, but because of the decentralized nature, it does have a wider range of interests than just what's on the news and inane politics. If you want to step away from established social media but can't break away from the format, this might be for you.
### Gemini Space
A number of services either host instant messaging through some extensions or sub-capsule hosting. I used Sourcehut's Gemini hosting until they terminated my account for not being a paying project maintainer. I'll have to find something else since the Flounder admin hasn't gotten back to me yet. Maybe they're busy.
## Services I learned about
### GNU Social
GNU's attempt to decentralize the social media space started with this. I do not know how to access it at this time, maybe with a Linux-Libre distro. All I know is that GNU has been on the rise lately with Linux-Libre becoming more viable by the day.
### GNU/Consensus
This is the development brother to GNU Social, meant to coordinate the development of FOSS software. According to Redecentralize, it's meant to inform FOSS developers about interesting projects that could use their help in a decentralized social net.
### Netsukuku
Instead of providing a single service, Netsukuku provides the groundwork for people to publish services without relying on a central network. Its FOSS infrastructure allows you to find services of any kind without using a central DNS. Best of all, it does not require access to the standard internet if you can swing it.
